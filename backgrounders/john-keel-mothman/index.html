John Keel and The Mothman Prophecies: A Backgrounder

The journalist who, alongside Jacques Vallee, moved UFO writing away from spaceships and toward a shape-shifting phenomenon tied to folklore, deception and the mind of the investigator. Names link to the UFO Researcher Network map. See also the UAP Desk.

Last updated Oct 30, 2026 (Jerusalem time). Facts marked unconfirmed were not checked against a second source.

1. Short bio

John A. Keel was born Alva John Kiehle on March 25, 1930, in Hornell, New York, and died of congestive heart failure on July 3, 2009, at Mount Sinai Hospital in Manhattan, at 79. As a boy he was fascinated by stage magic. After Army service, which ended in 1954, he wandered the Middle East and Asia writing for men's adventure magazines, and turned that trip into Jadoo (1957), a book on Eastern magic and fakirs. In the 1960s he was a television writer, including head writer for the game show Play Your Hunch. In 1966 Playboy commissioned a UFO article from him; it was rejected and the assignment went to J. Allen Hynek, whose piece ran in December 1967. Keel stayed with the subject, wrote dozens of articles and a run of books, and in 1987 revived the New York Fortean Society. His Army work in radio and television unconfirmed (from a friend's biography only).

2. Point Pleasant and the Silver Bridge, 1966–67

On the night of November 15, 1966, two young couples, Roger and Linda Scarberry and Steve and Mary Mallette, drove through the "TNT area," a former World War II munitions site about six miles north of Point Pleasant, West Virginia. They reported a gray, man-sized winged figure with large glowing red eyes that seemed to follow their car without flapping its wings. A newspaper copy editor named it "Mothman," a play on Batman. Sightings were reported for about 13 months, along with UFO reports, strange lights and visits by odd strangers.

On December 15, 1967, the Silver Bridge between Point Pleasant and Gallipolis, Ohio, collapsed into the Ohio River during rush-hour traffic, killing 46 people. Investigators traced the failure to a cracked eyebar in the suspension chain, not to anything paranormal. Keel made repeated trips to Point Pleasant from 1966 on, working with local reporter Mary Hyre.

3. The book (1975) and the film (2002)

The Mothman Prophecies was published by Saturday Review Press in 1975. It is less a monster book than a first-person account of an investigator being pulled into the events he studies: prophecies that came true or failed, calls and visits from strange people, and a growing sense that something was staging events for him. The creature takes up a small part of it.

The 2002 film, starring Richard Gere and Alan Bates, loosely adapts the book. Gere plays a fictional reporter, and Bates plays a researcher who carries Keel's ideas; obituaries describe the two as playing two parts of Keel's personality. Keel liked being "played" by Gere, and the film brought the book back into print. Director Mark Pellington unconfirmed.

4. Ultraterrestrials and the superspectrum

In UFOs: Operation Trojan Horse (Putnam, 1970), Keel argued that UFO reports belong to a much older run of strange events: airships in 1896–97, fairies and elves, demons, apparitions and séance spirits. His thesis was that one unknown source has always disguised itself in whatever form a culture expects, so in the space age it looks like spaceships. The "Trojan horse" is the disguise. He called the beings behind it "ultraterrestrials": not visitors from other planets but intelligences native to Earth that exist outside normal perception. Coinage of the word in that book unconfirmed.

One correction to a common summary: the fuller "superspectrum" theory is in The Eighth Tower (1975), not Operation Trojan Horse. There Keel describes a continuum of energies beyond the visible part of the electromagnetic spectrum, from which these entities can briefly take physical form, and which he linked to religious and occult experience as well as UFOs.

5. Men in Black, Indrid Cold, and the phone

Keel's Point Pleasant years were full of what he took to be manipulation aimed at him. He reported cars that vanished on back roads, nonsense messages left in hotel rooms, interference on his phone and mail, and calls from people who knew things they should not. He wrote about "Men in Black," odd, ill-fitting strangers who questioned witnesses. The term itself was popularized earlier, by Gray Barker's 1956 book They Knew Too Much About Flying Saucers.

Indrid Cold was the spaceman from the planet "Lanulos" whom West Virginia salesman Woodrow Derenberger said he met in 1966. Keel treated Cold as one more mask of the ultraterrestrials. Barker, in his own book, treated Cold as a product of Derenberger's storytelling. Vallee's journal from the period records receiving a "strange letter" from Keel in July 1967 that worried him with its "disturbing, prophetic style," and a friend who has edited Keel's papers notes that others were also concerned about him at that time.

6. Keel and Vallee

The parallels are strong. Working separately in the late 1960s, both rejected the "nuts-and-bolts" spaceship idea. Vallee's Passport to Magonia (1969) and Keel's Operation Trojan Horse (1970) both lined up UFO encounters with fairy lore and demon lore. Both saw deception at the center: Keel's Trojan horse and Vallee's later "control system" and Messengers of Deception (1979). The difference is emphasis. Vallee describes a system that shapes human belief, without saying who runs it. Keel names an intelligence and calls it hostile or at least indifferent. The comparison of the two models is my own analysis.

They did know each other. Vallee's published journal, Forbidden Science, records Keel's 1967 letter, and a 1985 photo shows the two side by side at a conference in San José, Costa Rica. Keel's literary executor says they did not get along well and that Keel suspected Vallee of taking his ideas unconfirmed (one source). In 2026 Diana Pasulka called Keel "a pal of Jacques Vallée's" on the Joe Rogan show.

7. Psychology: the investigator drawn in

The most useful thing in Keel, for the questions this Desk tracks, is what the subject did to him. His book shows a researcher whose attention seems to feed the events: the more he looked, the more coincidences, calls and prophecies came, until he could no longer tell staging from his own pattern-making. A skeptic reads this as paranoia and confirmation bias; Keel read it as the phenomenon playing with him. Both readings point to the same thing: the observer becomes part of the event.

George P. Hansen, a parapsychologist who was a friend of Keel's, built on Keel and Vallee in The Trickster and the Paranormal. Hansen argues that paranormal and UFO events cluster at boundaries and in times of change, mix real events with hoaxes, and resist being pinned down, like the trickster figures of myth. Gray Barker, a hoaxer who also investigated Point Pleasant, is almost a living example. Book year 2001 unconfirmed; that Hansen uses Keel's Mothman case as a case study unconfirmed (from the book's site description, which did not load for checking).

8. Pasulka, Kripal, and non-human intelligence today

Jeffrey Kripal wrote a chapter titled "On the Mothman, God, and Other Monsters: The Demonology of John A. Keel" (in Histories of the Hidden God, 2016), calling Keel a "self-described demonologist" and placing him inside an American living mythology mixing pulp fiction, science fiction and the space race. In a 2011 interview Kripal told scholars of religion to read The Mothman Prophecies.

On Joe Rogan #2533 (July 30, 2026), at about 29:55 by an unofficial transcript, Pasulka said Keel "thinks they're pretty nasty," called him "a doomer with respect to aliens," and recommended the film for anyone who wants an idea of "the super spectrum." Her current framing of non-human intelligence that works through technology fits Keel's idea of an intelligence that takes whatever form the era offers. I found no documented statement by Keel about artificial intelligence; the link to AI talk is my analysis: if the phenomenon wears the costume of each age, the costume of this age is the machine.

9. Criticisms

  • Skeptic Joe Nickell visited Point Pleasant and argued that the original sightings were real but were probably of a large owl, most likely a barred owl, whose eyes shine red in headlights. An Ohio professor at the time suggested a sandhill crane.
  • A folklore study argues that Keel's book turned a real disaster with 46 dead into a monster legend, and that the town's Mothman tourism now overshadows the victims.
  • Keel was a first-person storyteller with no way to check much of what he reported, and his friend Barker openly mixed fiction into his own Mothman book. Neither is a reliable record of events.
  • The ultraterrestrial idea cannot be tested: any evidence, including its absence, can be read as more deception.
