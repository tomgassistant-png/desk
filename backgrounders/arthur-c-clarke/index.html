Arthur C. Clarke: A Backgrounder

Why a science fiction writer keeps coming up in talk about the nature of the UFO phenomenon, consciousness, and artificial intelligence. Names link to the UFO Researcher Network map. See also the UAP Desk.

Last updated Oct 30, 2026 (Jerusalem time). Facts marked unconfirmed were not checked against a second source.

1. Short bio

Arthur C. Clarke was born in Minehead, England, in 1917 and died in Colombo, Sri Lanka, in 2008. He worked on radar for the Royal Air Force in World War II. In a 1945 article in Wireless World he proposed communication satellites in geostationary orbit. He moved to Ceylon (now Sri Lanka) in 1956 and lived there the rest of his life. He wrote about 100 books, fiction and nonfiction, and was knighted (announced 1998, conferred 2000) unconfirmed.

2. Childhood's End (1953)

Alien "Overlords" arrive just as the world is near nuclear war. They end war and poverty and run a peaceful world, but they hide their bodies for decades. When their leader, Karellen, finally appears, he looks exactly like the devil of medieval art. Most people barely react, because the Overlords' long rule through technology and global media has reshaped what people remember and fear.

The Overlords turn out to be servants of the "Overmind," a cosmic group mind. Their job is to prepare humanity's children, who develop psychic powers, to be absorbed into it. The "childhood" that ends is the human species itself. The children merge into the Overmind, and the Earth is destroyed. The book presents this as transcendence, but many readers find it closer to an apocalypse. One detail matters for this desk: the Overlords are sent to stop humans from studying the paranormal scientifically.

What to notice: intelligences that guide people through technology, a phenomenon that hides its form until people are ready, and an ending that is both "religious" and inhuman.

3. 2001: A Space Odyssey and HAL (1968)

Clarke and Stanley Kubrick built the film and the novel together, starting from Clarke's short story "The Sentinel" (written 1948, published 1951). Both came out in 1968. An alien monolith appears at two turning points in human evolution: when apes learn to use tools, and when humans reach out into space. Between those two points sits HAL 9000, the ship's computer, which turns on its crew. At the end, astronaut Dave Bowman is changed by the alien intelligence into the "Star Child."

This is the convergence Pasulka stresses: artificial intelligence and alien intelligence appear together at a decisive moment in human evolution (per Rod Dreher's review of her book).

4. Clarke's Third Law

"Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic." It first appeared in a 1968 letter to Science and was added to the 1973 revised edition of his essay collection Profiles of the Future. For UFO thinkers it is a bridge: if a non-human intelligence is advanced enough, its contact could look like the supernatural, which is close to the reasoning behind Jacques Vallee's link between UFO reports, fairy lore and religious visions.

5. Teilhard and the noosphere

Jesuit paleontologist Pierre Teilhard de Chardin (1881–1955) argued that evolution is moving toward a planet-wide "sphere of mind," the noosphere. The Overmind in Childhood's End is often read as a science fiction version of this idea.

Caution: a direct influence on Clarke is not documented in what was checked. Childhood's End (1953) came out before Teilhard's main book, The Phenomenon of Man, appeared (1955). The documented influence on Clarke is the British writer Olaf Stapledon. The Teilhard link is a parallel that commentators, including Pasulka, draw. It should not be read as Clarke's own source unconfirmed.

6. Clarke's own views on UFOs

  • He saw some. In The View from Serendip (1977) he wrote: "Of the half-dozen UFOs I have seen…" He then said these experiences taught him how "unusual circumstances, inaccurate information, and heightened emotional states can completely delude observers," and that UFOs as interstellar spaceships could no longer be taken very seriously. Aliens, he added, "won't hang around for centuries, looking for a place to park."
  • He hosted the mysteries. Arthur C. Clarke's Mysterious World (1980, 13 episodes) had a UFO episode in which he says he has been interested for 50 years and has seen at least six. He kept his opinion open early in the episode.
  • He ended up a near-total skeptic. After that series and World of Strange Powers (1985), he reportedly called himself "an almost total skeptic" unconfirmed (found only on Wikipedia).
  • The often-repeated line that he had seen UFOs but none stayed unexplained matches the substance of the 1977 passage, but the exact wording was not found unconfirmed.

So Clarke is a paradox for this desk: in his own life he treated sightings as perception errors, a psychological story, while his fiction gave the modern imagination its strongest picture of a hidden non-human intelligence steering humanity.

7. Machine intelligence and merging with machines

Clarke often wrote that thinking machines might be humanity's successors, and that humans might move their minds into machines. Profiles of the Future has a chapter on this (often quoted as "The Obsolescence of Man") unconfirmed. In 2001's novel, the aliens themselves are said to have passed from biological bodies to machine bodies and then to pure energy, which ties AI and alien intelligence into one evolutionary path unconfirmed.

9. Vallee, Hynek and the control system (analysis)

No direct working tie between Clarke and Vallee or J. Allen Hynek was found. The overlap is in ideas, and what follows is this page's analysis, not a documented connection.

In The Invisible College (1975) Vallee proposed that UFOs may be a "control system," like a thermostat, that conditions human belief over centuries. In Messengers of Deception (1979) he warned that belief in benevolent space visitors could be used to manipulate people. The Overlords fit both ideas closely: a hidden intelligence that shapes human culture, reveals itself on a schedule, and prepares people for a change they cannot judge. Vallee himself says he does not mean literal angels or demons. Clarke's book makes the demon image literal. Dreher writes that Pasulka "lands where Jacques Vallée does": whatever this is, it is "engaged in a project of control."

10. Today's AI-as-alien-intelligence talk

Current debate often describes large AI systems as a non-human or "alien" intelligence that people summon through technology, which is the 2001 pattern. Pasulka's The Others argues that AI, UFOs and global media belong to one story about forces shaping human perception. On the UAP Desk feed, Avi Loeb and Bernardo Kastrup discuss Vallee and whether AI can be conscious. Clarke supplies the shared picture behind much of this: a superior intelligence that works through machines and media, and that may look like magic, or like a god or a devil.
