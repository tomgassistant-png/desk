France presidential election

Daily research digest section · latest edition 20 October 2026

Tracker for the French presidential election of April–May 2027: dates, candidates, polls, Polymarket odds, and news that affects the political situation in France (the government, the budget, censure motions, candidate moves and court cases). First edition: 16 October 2026, covering about 2 to 16 October 2026.

20 October 2026 · today

NewNuñez offers to resign and Macron refuses; first local curfews in Paris, Lyon, Nice, the Lille area and Bordeaux

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez offered his resignation after Macron's address on the evening of 18 October, and the Élysée said on Monday morning that the president refused it because of 'the need for continuous command of the police,' while asking for 'new methods of action' against rioters (franceinfo; BFMTV; Le Parisien). Nuñez told France Inter, 'I am responsible for everything that happens,' and admitted a 'lack of anticipation' and a 'loss of control' in some cities; Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin said on France 2, 'We incontestably failed' (TF1 Info live). After a crisis meeting Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu announced three decisions: a 'massive' penal response, partial and local curfews ordered by prefects, and support for businesses, with 100 percent state-paid partial unemployment and a special fund; the government says at least 3,000 shops were damaged or looted over the weekend. He said he would not put all of Paris under curfew and that a state of emergency remains possible 'if it is useful.' The curfews on Monday night: in Paris, the central esplanade of the Place de la République and streets leading into Les Halles from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m., with demonstrations banned from 8 p.m. in République, Châtelet, Bastille, the main railway stations, the Champs-Élysées and around the Élysée, Matignon and Parliament; in Lyon, the city center from 9 p.m. to 6 a.m.; in Nice, a curfew for minors from 9 p.m. until 25 October, ordered by Mayor Éric Ciotti; in Lille, Douai, Denain and Wattignies, a curfew for unaccompanied minors from 8 p.m. to 6 a.m. on Monday and Tuesday nights; and in Bordeaux, for unaccompanied minors from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m., from 20 to 26 October (Le Parisien; TF1 Info; franceinfo). Le Parisien's reporters described a calm first night in Paris, with gendarmes sealing the Forum des Halles and a police helicopter overhead; ABC of Madrid also reported calm. In Lyon, about 100 members of the ultra-right joined a rally in support of shopkeepers and chanted slogans such as 'Islam out of Europe' (Le Progrès, as quoted by Le Parisien). Paris Mayor Emmanuel Grégoire said the curfew 'must remain exceptional.'

NewCourts: 2,513 people in custody and 67 jailed; looters to be charged as organized crime; antiterrorism prosecutors weigh the Limoges fire

Darmanin said on TF1's evening news on 19 October that 2,513 people had been placed in police custody since Saturday and 67 had already been jailed, 34 of them in the Aix-Marseille appeals court area, with sentences of up to five years; he has told all prosecutors to treat looters as organized crime, which allows custody of up to 72 hours instead of 48 and the use of phone taps and location tracking (TF1 Info; Le Parisien). He said the national antiterrorism prosecutor's office is 'observing' the burning of the Limoges town hall and will decide within hours whether to take the case; about 5,000 people, 700 of them described by the authorities as 'radicalized and extremely violent,' had marched in Limoges (TF1 Info; not independently confirmed). Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau said 557 people were held after the first day, 433 of them adults, and that most of those whose cases have been decided were little known to the police; she said she 'absolutely cannot confirm' that those arrested belong to the ultra-left, though black bloc groups were in the march from the start (TF1 Info; La Provence). A Paris police official, Michel Lavaud, said only 20 percent of those arrested in the Paris area since Saturday live in Paris (Le Parisien). Housing Minister Vincent Jeanbrun announced on X a circular with Darmanin to withhold from offenders' social benefits sums equal to the proceeds of looting or receiving stolen goods. Overseas, about 50 people were arrested after burning roadblocks and looting in Guadeloupe and Martinique (TF1 Info, from AFP). Coming days: Lecornu receives the unions on Wednesday morning and visits Limoges on Friday; a coordination of gilets jaunes has called an 'Act II' of protests for Saturday, 24 October, and Nuñez promised Marseille a larger police deployment that day; the CGT, which accuses the government of a 'strategy of chaos,' keeps its call for strikes and marches on 5 November (franceinfo; CNews; Le Parisien).

NewWho is behind the riots: Lecornu points to an ultra-left network aiming at the election; Mediapart and LFI say intelligence officers were told to blame LFI

Lecornu said on 19 October, citing the intelligence services, that an ultra-left movement wants to organize 'deep disorder' that could call into question the calendar of the presidential election; he met the heads of the DGSI and other intelligence services with Nuñez that evening (franceinfo; Le Parisien). Mélenchon replied on X that as a candidate he had heard no one speak of this 'in private or in public,' and asked which group Lecornu meant. That evening he shared a Mediapart report, based on police sources, that a superior in the intelligence services told officers to write that La France insoumise was behind the movement; Mediapart's account has not been independently confirmed, and L'Opinion, citing a police source, reported the opposite view, that LFI helped plan the disorder. The facts so far do not settle the question: Paris prosecutor Beccuau said she cannot confirm that those arrested are from the ultra-left, and the DGSI denied a claim, made by a Journal du Dimanche journalist on CNews, that Kalashnikov rifles and submachine guns had been found on rioters (Le Parisien, citing BFMTV and Le Monde). On the far right, Éric Zemmour said on LCI on 18 October that the riots showed 'the true face of the great replacement,' describing 'a horde of North African and African barbarians'; LFI deputy Antoine Léaument has referred the remarks to the prosecutor. President Trump said in the United States that the unrest is 'linked to immigration, not to schools,' and the US Embassy advised Americans to avoid protest areas (TF1 Info, from AFP).

NewCandidates compete on order: Bardella promises penal majority at 16, Glucksmann calls Mélenchon 'the stepping stone of the far right,' Attal wants social networks blocked

The candidates spent 19 October setting out how they would restore order (the effect on voting intentions has not yet been measured by any poll taken after 17 October). Jordan Bardella, at a National Rally press conference titled 'Facing chaos, the France of order,' called the weekend 'a political and security failure' and said that, if elected, the RN would lower the age of criminal majority to 16, suspend family allowances for families of repeat offenders, make looters pay for damage, set minimum sentences and give police more staff and equipment ('you touch a police officer, you sleep in prison'); he said neither he nor Marine Le Pen would attend Lecornu's meeting of party leaders, to which the RN will send a vice president (TF1 Info; CNews; RN). Raphaël Glucksmann, at the Paris town hall rally and on RTL, called for an 'extremely firm' response and said Mélenchon, by failing to condemn the violence clearly, is 'the stepping stone of the far right,' adding, 'Who will benefit from this chaos if not Mrs. Le Pen?' (France 24, from AFP; BFMTV; Le Parisien). Gabriel Attal (Renaissance) said 'the authority of the state has failed' and proposed temporarily blocking some social networks during exceptional events; Bruno Retailleau (LR) said a curfew is 'insufficient,' asked again for a state of emergency and proposed stripping dual nationals convicted of such violence of French citizenship; David Lisnard, mayor of Cannes and president of the mayors' association, said 'the state has become a fiction'; Marion Maréchal said Macron should consider resigning (TF1 Info). The mayors' association, which held rallies at town halls at noon, called the attacks 'premeditated' and asked for a change in police doctrine. Separately, the former prime minister François Fillon endorsed Retailleau on 17 October (La Dépêche).

NewBudget: the Assembly suspends the vote on the revenue part and moves to the social security budget; minister says the debate has added €10 billion to the deficit

The solemn vote on the first (revenue) part of the 2027 state budget, planned for Tuesday 20 October, has been postponed: the National Assembly stopped debating it at midnight on Monday with more than 2,600 amendments still to examine, and takes up the social security budget from Tuesday afternoon, as the constitutional deadlines require. Debate on the state budget is due to resume on 27 October (LCP, from AFP; Capital, citing Le Monde and the Ministry for Relations with Parliament). Public Accounts Minister David Amiel said on Monday evening that the first week of debate had worsened the public deficit by €10 billion; deputies have rejected a key article on the tax allowance for pensions and much of a section on savings in local-government funding. The government's target is a deficit of 5 percent of GDP in 2027, against 5.4 percent in 2026. LCP expects the revenue part to be rejected in the end, which would send the bill to the Senate; Lecornu can still use Article 49.3, at the risk of a censure motion, or, from 18 December, budget ordinances, which have never been used.

19 October 2026

Macron speaks of "anger and shame," promises the looters will pay, but declares no state of emergency and offers no new measures

President Macron addressed the nation for about nine minutes at 8 p.m. Paris time on 18 October (9 p.m. in Israel), saying, "We all feel anger and shame tonight." He blamed the violence of 17 October on "agitators from ultra-left groups, joined by many delinquents" and said "nothing explains it, nothing excuses it"; those who destroyed "must pay," those who looted "must repay," and the state will be "intractable." He said prefects, together with mayors, may go as far as banning some gatherings, ordered "transparency" on the policing strategy and told Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez to "draw all the lessons" on intelligence and anticipation. A crisis cell will help shopkeepers and local governments from Monday. He asked parties and unions to condemn the violence "without ambiguity" and said, "The country needs order... not escalation" and "Our country deserves a fine presidential election in six months." He said he understood high school pupils and students and that a youth consultation platform has received more than 100,000 contributions, but he announced no new cost-of-living measures; the prime minister is to meet parties, unions and local officials. He did not declare a state of emergency, which the right had demanded, and he keeps his trip to four Gulf countries from Monday to Wednesday. France 24, citing AFP, noted that he said nothing about the protesters who were badly injured.

17 October final count: 550,000 marchers by police count, 1,200 arrests, 150 police injured, Limoges town hall burned

Saturday 17 October: a 'convergence' day against the cost of living, with bans in central Paris

Pupils, so-called students, former gilets jaunes, climate activists, the CGT-led union alliance and the Confédération paysanne have called for a joint day of protest against the cost of living on Saturday, 17 October; the main farmers' unions, the FNSEA and the Coordination rurale, are not taking part. The Paris police prefect, Patrice Faure, banned demonstrations around the Élysée, Matignon, the Champs-Élysées, Concorde, Bastille and other sites (fine €135); the authorized route runs from République to Place de Clichy, which the student collective Interfac IDF refused, and many metro stations are closed. The stun grenades suspended for the school protests (after a 15-year-old lost a hand in Lens on 5 October) may be used again in case of 'extremely serious urban violence'; the teachers' union SNES-FSU called this irresponsible. Lecornu set up an interministerial crisis cell, Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin told prosecutors to prepare special court arrangements, and President Macron, in Brussels, appealed for calm. The government also extended to the end of the year a €100 aid for people who drive long distances to work. The website of the Republicans (LR) was hacked by activists calling for the protests; an LR source said no data was leaked.

Opinion and analysis: 69 percent approve of the 17 October protest; an essayist doubts a repeat of 2018

An Elabe poll for BFMTV (1,506 people, 14–16 October) finds that 69 percent of the French approve of the mobilization against the cost of living (36 percent support it and 33 percent feel sympathy; 14 percent are indifferent, 10 percent opposed and 7 percent hostile). Approval is 83 percent among left-wing (NFP) voters, 70 percent among National Rally voters, 55 percent among Republican voters and 51 percent among voters of Macron's camp, and 77 percent among people aged 18 to 24. Sixty-five percent say people are right to protest now rather than wait for the vote in April. AFP also cites 55 percent support for the high-school and student movement (71 percent among 18- to 24-year-olds). On the candidates: Marine Le Pen (CNews, 15 October) called for 'total transparency' in the criteria of Parcoursup, the university admissions system; Édouard Philippe proposed school days focused on the morning and shorter Toussaint and Easter holidays, and defended Parcoursup. The government says 2,000 of the 3,000 promised substitute teachers have been assigned (Education Ministry via AFP, which confirms the point reported earlier by TF1 alone) and promises Parcoursup measures before the end of October. Analysis: the essayist Jean-Laurent Cassely told RTL on 16 October that he does not expect a repeat of the 2018 gilets jaunes movement; he said the gilets jaunes and the pupils, though marching side by side, come from different social and cultural worlds, and their votes will not express the same anger.

Protest day: clashes in Paris, Lyon, Bordeaux, Marseille and Lille; 272 arrests by 3 p.m.

The 17 October day of protest against the cost of living brought tens of thousands into the streets and violent clashes in several cities. At noon the Interior Ministry counted 140 actions outside Paris with 32,800 people. By 3 p.m. police had made 272 arrests nationwide, according to the ministry, BFMTV and Reuters; 133 of them were in Paris by 2 p.m., mostly for 'gathering with intent to commit violence.' In Paris, the police prefecture said several hundred people, 'mainly from the ultra-left,' attacked police at Place de la République from 1 p.m.; police used tear gas and a water cannon, and the authorized CGT march left for Place de Clichy at 2:30. An undeclared march toward the Élysée was turned back. A car and a newspaper kiosk were set on fire at Place de la République, AP reported, as carried by France 24. In Marseille, dozens forced their way into the city hall lobby, and a municipal police station was attacked; Mayor Benoît Payan demanded an account of the policing. In Lyon, a branch of the tourist office was ransacked and set on fire while firefighters were kept away. In Bordeaux, a march of several thousand turned into clashes. In Lille, several hundred people fought riot police beside a peaceful march, and some briefly broke into city hall. AFP, carried by Europe 1, reported about 20,000 marchers each in Lyon and Nantes and 10,000 in Rennes at 3 p.m., without naming the source of the count. The unions and organizers had not published a national figure by early evening, and the ministry had not published a final one; the organizers had expected 60,000 to 110,000. Jordan Bardella said only 'professionals of chaos' were in the streets; Marine Le Pen said the left had taken the protests hostage. Jean-Luc Mélenchon said tens of thousands marched calmly while television showed only violence. The police inspectorate is examining at least 24 complaints of police violence, Reuters reports.

16 October 2026

School protests: a mainly high-school movement, now in its fourth week, with about 8,000 people taken into custody

The movement is real and large, but it is chiefly a movement of lycéens (high school pupils, mostly aged 15 to 18), not of university students, although some universities, parents and unions have joined. It began in mid-September in the Paris suburbs (reports name Créteil and Seine-Saint-Denis) over teacher shortages, overcrowded classes and dilapidated buildings, and spread to schools across France through class strikes, blockades of school entrances and marches. Scale: the Interior Ministry counted more than 250,000 marchers on 6 October, about 71,500 on 8 October and 161,500 on 13 October (41,500 in Paris); unions claimed about 480,000 on 13 October (one source; unconfirmed). On 6 October the government reported disruption at 891 high schools, almost a quarter of all lycées; about 200 were still blockaded on 14 October. Violence: most marches have been peaceful, but clashes at the end of several marches in Paris (Bastille on 8 October, Place Vauban on 13 October), Marseille, Lyon, Nantes and elsewhere involved fireworks, stones and burning cars on one side and tear gas, water cannon and stun grenades on the other. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu told the Senate on 14 October that 937 police and gendarmes, 149 school staff and 248 pupils had been injured since the start. The Justice Ministry reported 7,950 people taken into custody (6,773 minors), 2,735 prosecuted and 26 held in pretrial detention. A 15-year-old lost a hand to a police stun grenade and a pupil in Tours lost an eye; police officers have been charged in that case and in a case at Saint-Ouen-l'Aumône, and 36 internal police inquiries are open.

16 October 2026 edition · franceinfo (custody figures) · TF1 Info (Lecornu's injury figures) · The Guardian · Euronews · BBC · AP · CNews (8 October) · Human Rights Watch

Who backs the protests, what each side says, and how the government has responded

Protesters and their supporters say the cause is years of underinvestment in schools, and many denounce police tactics; the CGT and student unions have called further days of action. Human Rights Watch said on 14 October that the protests were overwhelmingly peaceful and that police used excessive force against children. Critics, including the government, say a violent minority, not all of them pupils, attacks police and schools, and that La France insoumise (LFI) encourages the unrest. Lecornu spoke of 'unprecedented violence.' On 1 October Bruno Retailleau, the Republicans' candidate, said the question of banning LFI 'arises' because it 'calls for sedition'; Jordan Bardella of the National Rally proposed that the cost of the damage be split between the families of arsonists and LFI. Jean-Luc Mélenchon replied that there had been 'neither a leaflet, nor a speech, nor a call' from LFI to blockade any school, called it 'the first mass movement summoned by telepathy,' and accused Marine Le Pen's 'friends in the police' of violence. Government response: the use of one type of stun grenade was suspended after the injury of the 15-year-old, and the government says it will speed the assignment of substitute teachers (the second point is reported by TF1 only; unconfirmed).

Public opinion and possible effect on the 2027 election: support for the pupils, rejection of LFI's role

Polls taken from 1 to 9 October agree that about half or more of the French back the pupils but want the protests to end. Elabe for BFMTV (6–7 October): 55 percent approve, 72 percent call the demands legitimate, 55 percent want the movement to stop. Cluster17 for L'Opinion (7–9 October): 57 percent support, 53 percent want it to end as soon as possible. Ifop for LCI (6–7 October): 48 percent support or sympathize, below the 57 percent recorded for the 2023 pension protests. Ipsos: 54 percent approve. Odoxa for Le Figaro (1 October): 62 percent find the movement justified, but 61 percent say LFI is out of its role and 78 percent call its involvement electoral. Support is sharply divided by party (above 80 percent on the left; about a third among RN voters; under a quarter among LR voters) and by age (above 70 percent among the young). Analysts quoted by Le Dauphiné (the sociologists Camille Peugny and Vincent Tiberj) doubt either a large rightward backlash or a large surge in young left-wing voters; Tiberj noted that voting is no longer the main outlet for many young people. The Soufan Center argues that the protests have raised Mélenchon's standing among young voters and could matter for whether he reaches the second round. The first-round polls have not shifted visibly: a Toluna Harris Interactive poll for RTL (12 October) put Le Pen at 35 to 37 percent. On Polymarket on 16 October, Le Pen stood at about 46 percent and Mélenchon at about 11.5; I have not measured how these odds moved during the protests.

Glucksmann wins the left's primary in the first round

On 11 October Raphaël Glucksmann, leader of Place publique, won the joint primary of the Socialist Party, Place publique and the Gauche républicaine et socialiste in the first round, with 66.17 percent (79,613 votes). Olivier Faure, first secretary of the Socialist Party, had 14.46 percent, Ségolène Royal 7.62, Emmanuel Maurel 5.92 and Jérôme Guedj 5.83; 120,787 people voted. The vote was extended by a day after a cyberattack slowed the online platform. For the first time, the Socialist Party will not have one of its own members as its presidential candidate. Glucksmann invited François Hollande, who stayed out of the primary, to join him, and called on the whole left except La France insoumise to unite.

16 October 2026 edition · L'Opinion · Public Sénat (Odoxa) · Commission des sondages

2027 budget clears its first vote with help from the National Rally

On 13 October the National Assembly rejected the Socialists' preliminary motion to reject the 2027 budget, 236 votes to 149; the National Rally voted with the government. Had it passed, the bill would have gone to the Senate without debate in the Assembly. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu had written to the Socialists that morning asking them to withdraw the motion; they refused, saying that he will not make the richest pay more. Lecornu says this budget should be "as reversible as possible" and that tax questions belong to the presidential campaign. The Socialists have not ruled out a censure motion.

16 October 2026 edition · Le Monde · HuffPost France · 20 Minutes

Lecornu presents a budget without a majority and hopes for a compromise by December

Lecornu presented the 2027 budget to the Council of Ministers on 1 October, as last year without a majority in Parliament. He has three ways to pass it without a vote: Article 49.3, which would expose the government to a censure motion that needs 289 votes; budget ordinances under Article 47, never used, and not possible before 18 December; or a special law that carries the budget past 31 December. On 15 October he said that he expects to reach a compromise with members of Parliament by December. The Assembly continued to debate the revenue side that day.
