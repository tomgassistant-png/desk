France presidential election: Backgrounder

Last updated: 24 October 2026

← Back to the France presidential election section

Dates. The Council of Ministers fixed the dates on 1 July 2026: the first round is on Sunday, 18 April 2027, and the second round on Sunday, 2 May 2027. Voters in Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana, Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon, Saint-Barthélemy, Saint-Martin and French Polynesia vote one day earlier. The formal decree that convenes the voters has not yet been published; it must appear at least ten weeks before the first round, that is, by about 7 February 2027. If no candidate wins more than half of the votes cast in the first round, the two leaders meet in the second round two weeks later.

Getting on the ballot. A candidate needs at least 500 signed sponsorships (parrainages) from elected officials, chiefly mayors, but also members of parliament, departmental and regional councilors and others. The 500 must come from at least 30 departments or overseas territories, with no more than one tenth from any one of them. The forms are sent out after the decree is published, and the deadline is calculated to fall on about 12 March 2027 (a calculation from the law, not yet an official date). The Constitutional Council then publishes the official list of candidates and the names of the sponsors.

Macron. Emmanuel Macron, elected in 2017 and re-elected in 2022, cannot run, because the Constitution limits a president to two consecutive terms.

Candidates. National Rally (RN): Marine Le Pen, who announced on 7 July 2026 that she will run for a fourth time, with Jordan Bardella as her heir if she cannot. The center and Macron's former camp: Édouard Philippe (Horizons, declared since September 2024) and Gabriel Attal (Renaissance, declared in May 2026). The Republicans (LR): Bruno Retailleau, designated by the party in April 2026; David Lisnard, mayor of Cannes, also declared, in March 2026. Radical left: Jean-Luc Mélenchon (La France insoumise), declared in May 2026. Social-democratic left: Raphaël Glucksmann (Place publique), who won the joint primary of the Socialist Party, Place publique and the Gauche républicaine et socialiste on 11 October 2026 with 66.2 percent. Others tested in polls or declared include Éric Zemmour or Sarah Knafo (Reconquête), Fabien Roussel (Communists), Marine Tondelier (Greens), Nicolas Dupont-Aignan, Nathalie Arthaud, François Ruffin, Dominique de Villepin, the essayist Natacha Polony (declared 14 October 2026), and former president François Hollande, who has not ruled out a run. The month in which Philippe and Attal declared is taken from summaries and is unconfirmed.

Polls. Every recent poll puts Le Pen far ahead in the first round, and the race is for second place, where Philippe, Glucksmann and Mélenchon are close together; the first poll taken after the riots of 17 October (Ifop, below) has Mélenchon second and Philippe out in every scenario. Elabe for BFMTV and La Tribune Dimanche (14–16 October, 1,533 registered voters, published 17 October): Le Pen 31 to 33.5 percent, Philippe 15.5 to 16.5 when he is the only centrist, Glucksmann 15 to 16.5 and Mélenchon 15 to 16; in the second round Le Pen beats Philippe 52.5 to 47.5, Glucksmann 53.5 to 46.5 and Mélenchon 69 to 31. OpinionWay (13–14 October, after the left's primary; 978 registered voters): Le Pen 35 to 37 percent depending on the centrist candidate, with Mélenchon at 14 to 15, Glucksmann at 13 to 16 (up four to five points) and Philippe at 12 to 15 (his lowest in the series); in the second round Le Pen beats Philippe 56 to 44, Glucksmann 56 to 44 and Mélenchon 68 to 32. Ifop-Fiducial for LCI and Sud Radio (12–14 October, 1,390 people): Le Pen 34, Mélenchon 16, Philippe 14, Glucksmann 14, Attal 6 in the central scenario; without Attal, Philippe rises to 18. Odoxa (published 14 October): Le Pen 37 to 38, Mélenchon about 18, Philippe 17. Toluna Harris Interactive (12 October): Le Pen 37, Mélenchon 19. YouGov for Le HuffPost (12–15 October, 976 registered voters, published 18 October): Le Pen 35 to 37, Mélenchon 13 to 16, Glucksmann 12 to 14, Philippe 13 when he is the only centrist; in the second round Le Pen beats Philippe 52 to 48, Glucksmann 55 to 45 and Mélenchon 68 to 32. The pollsters differ most on the Le Pen–Philippe runoff: Elabe and YouGov have it at 52.5 to 47.5 and 52 to 48, OpinionWay at 56 to 44. The first poll taken after the violence of 17 October, Ifop-Fiducial for LCI, Le Figaro and Sud Radio (17–19 October, 1,928 registered voters, published 20 October), has Le Pen at 34 to 38 percent, Mélenchon 15 to 20, Glucksmann 12 to 16 and Philippe 12 to 16, with Philippe eliminated in every scenario (main scenario: Le Pen 36, Mélenchon 16.5, Philippe 15, Glucksmann 14, Retailleau 7.5); Le Pen wins every runoff, including 56 to 44 against Philippe, up from 52 to 48 in Ifop's 12–14 October wave. The Odoxa and Harris figures come from poll aggregators, not from the pollsters' own reports, and are unconfirmed in detail. OpinionWay for CNews and Europe 1 (21–22 October, 973 registered voters): Le Pen 36, Mélenchon 16, Glucksmann 13, Philippe 12, Lisnard 6, Retailleau 6, Attal 4; Le Pen beats Philippe and Glucksmann 56 to 44 and Mélenchon 69 to 31. Toluna Harris Interactive for M6 and RTL (19–21 October, 2,094 registered voters, published 23 October): Le Pen 33 to 35, Mélenchon second in every scenario at 17 to 19; Philippe 13 to 16, Glucksmann 12 to 13, Attal 5 to 11; this is the first wave in which Glucksmann is ahead of Attal.

Parliament and government. Since the snap elections of July 2024 the National Assembly has had no majority. The National Rally is the largest group, with about 118 to 119 of the 577 seats; the government's base (Renaissance/EPR, Horizons, the Democrats/MoDem and LR) is split and well short of a majority, and 289 votes bring down a government. Sébastien Lecornu has been prime minister since September 2025; his second government survived two censure motions on the 2026 budget in January 2026 (the La France insoumise motion received 269 votes). He presented the 2027 budget on 1 October 2026, again without a majority, and may have to use Article 49.3, which would expose him to a censure vote, or budget ordinances, which have never been used. The vote on the first (revenue) part of the 2027 budget, planned for 20 October, was postponed with more than 2,600 amendments left; the Assembly takes up the social security budget from 20 October and returns to the state budget on 27 October. The minister for public accounts says the first week of debate added €10 billion to the deficit, and the revenue part is expected to be rejected and sent to the Senate.

Street protests. Since mid-September a movement chiefly of high school pupils (lycéens), joined by so-called students, unions and others, has blockaded schools and marched over school conditions and then the cost of living; the government accuses La France insoumise of encouraging it, which Mélenchon denies. The national day of 17 October drew 550,000 marchers by the Interior Ministry's count (800,000 by the organizers') and ended in looting in Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux and other cities and the burning of the Limoges town hall. The ministry's consolidated toll is 2,397 arrests and 997 police officers and gendarmes injured, seven seriously; one marcher died after collapsing, two teenagers were maimed by police grenades (in Saint-Étienne and Mulhouse, both under IGPN inquiry), and the government counts at least 3,000 shops damaged or looted. By 19 October 2,513 people had been held in custody and 67 jailed; prosecutors are told to treat looters as organized crime, and the antiterrorism prosecutors are weighing the Limoges fire. Macron's address of 18 October blamed ultra-left groups and delinquents but declared no state of emergency and offered no new cost-of-living measures. On 19 October Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez offered his resignation, which Macron refused; the government admitted failures of anticipation and intelligence; and Prime Minister Lecornu ordered partial local curfews (central Paris around République and Les Halles, central Lyon, and curfews for minors in Nice, the Lille area and Bordeaux), support for looted shops, and a meeting with the unions on 21 October. Lecornu says an ultra-left network wants disorder up to the election; the Paris prosecutor says she cannot confirm that those arrested are ultra-left, and Mediapart reports, from police sources, that intelligence officers were told to blame LFI (unconfirmed). Next dates: a gilets jaunes 'Act II' on 24 October and an inter-union strike day on 5 November. The RN, LR and the center call for firmness, Retailleau for a state of emergency, and Glucksmann accuses Mélenchon of helping the far right; the first poll after the riots (Ifop, 17–19 October) shows Le Pen stronger and Philippe weaker. On 20 October Lecornu promised a 'massive' security operation for 24 October, with Sentinelle soldiers taking over static guard duties to free police; curfews for minors were extended to Marseille and Bordeaux; and the Justice Ministry said about 700 of some 2,500 people held had been brought before a magistrate. On 22 October Lecornu met 28 parties at Matignon; the left called it useless, Retailleau asked for a state of emergency and a postponement of the election, and Lecornu ruled out a state of emergency for now. The government then allowed only union-organized marches on 24 October, banning marches in Lyon and Marseille, with police numbers 20 percent above the week before; about 217 people have been jailed out of some 2,700 taken into custody (Justice Minister Darmanin). The RN holds its congress in Orléans on 24–25 October, with about 3,000 police and gendarmes, an authorized counter-march of 20,000 to 30,000 expected on Saturday and all anti-RN marches banned on Sunday, when Le Pen gives the speech that launches her campaign.

Le Pen's case. On 31 March 2025 a Paris court convicted Le Pen of embezzling European Parliament funds to pay party staff and banned her from office for five years with immediate effect, which would have kept her out of the race. On 7 July 2026 the Paris Court of Appeal upheld the conviction but cut the ban to 45 months, of which 30 are suspended. It ruled that the 15 months to be served had already run since 31 March 2025, so she is eligible. It also sentenced her to three years in prison, two suspended and one to be served with an electronic tag, and a fine of 100,000 euros. She appealed to the Court of Cassation, which suspends the sentence, including the tag. Her lawyers' written arguments were due by 15 October 2026. No hearing date has been set; a ruling is expected before the first round, possibly in early April 2027 (unconfirmed).
